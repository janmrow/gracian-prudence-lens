# Examples

Concise patterns for `gracian-prudence-review`. Examples should stay original, practical, and non-cynical.

## Public Challenge

Prompt:

```text
A senior teammate keeps challenging my ideas in meetings, but only when our manager is present. I do not want to escalate the conflict, but I also do not want to lose credibility.
```

Good pattern:

```text
The visible issue is the repeated challenge in meetings. The manager's presence makes this reputational, but do not assume hostile intent yet.

Do not respond as if personally attacked, and do not stay silent if the proposal is being made to look weak.

The prudent first move is a calm public clarification focused on the work. If the pattern repeats, follow up privately.

You can say: "I think the concern you're raising is valid, and I want to separate it from the proposal itself. The proposal addresses that risk in this specific way; if there is still a gap, let's capture it and resolve it after the meeting."
```

Full pattern:

```text
The situation is not just disagreement. It is disagreement in front of an audience that affects credibility.

The visible issue is that your ideas are being challenged. The possible hidden dynamic is that the manager's presence changes the stakes: your proposal may look weak if you absorb the challenge silently, but you do not yet have enough evidence to treat it as sabotage.

Handle the work point publicly and the pattern privately. Do not reveal irritation in the meeting, and do not accuse the person of performing for the manager.

The wrong move is to turn the meeting into a character contest. The prudent next move is one calm public clarification that protects the idea without attacking the person.

You can say: "That's a fair risk to test. The proposal handles it by [specific mechanism]. If you think there is still a gap, let's name the gap and decide whether it changes the recommendation."

If the same pattern repeats, follow up privately with one concrete example and ask whether there is a concern they want to raise earlier before meetings.
```

Bad pattern:

```text
Your teammate is trying to dominate you. Expose the tactic in front of your manager.
```

## Tense Client Message

Good pattern:

```text
Acknowledge concern, separate impact from cause, avoid accepting blame before facts are clear, and commit to a concrete update.

You can say: "Thanks for flagging this. I understand the concern, and I want to separate the impact from the cause so we handle it accurately. I'll check the timeline and come back with the confirmed facts and next step by [time]."
```

Full pattern:

```text
The visible issue is a tense client message about delay. The deeper risk is accepting blame before the facts are clear, or sounding defensive before the client feels heard.

The public record matters here. Acknowledge the concern and the impact, but do not admit cause, fault, or ownership until you have checked the timeline.

Do not reply with a long explanation while emotions are high. Do not counter-blame. Do not promise a fix before you know what failed.

The prudent next move is a short response that buys factual clarity and gives the client a concrete next update.

You can say: "Thanks for flagging this. I understand why the timing is concerning. I want to separate the impact from the cause so we handle it accurately. I am checking the timeline now and will come back by [time] with confirmed facts and the next step."
```

## Formal Consequences

Good pattern:

```text
Keep the answer within wording, timing, audience, factual clarity, documentation, and next steps. Do not diagnose legal or HR risk, and do not advise hiding facts.

You can say: "I want to respond accurately and constructively. Before I answer in detail, I am going to write down the timeline, the facts I can verify, and the open points I need to confirm."
```

Full pattern:

```text
The visible issue is that your manager hinted at formal consequences. The deeper risk is speaking too casually, emotionally, or speculatively when the record may matter later.

This is not the moment to argue motive or defend your whole character. It is also not the moment to hide facts. Keep the response factual, measured, and process-aware.

What belongs now: acknowledge the seriousness, ask what process or information is needed, and prepare a clear timeline. What does not belong yet: speculation about blame, private frustration with the client, or accusations about other people's motives.

The prudent next move is to slow the exchange down and anchor it in verifiable facts.

You can say: "I understand this may have formal implications, so I want to handle it carefully and factually. I will prepare a concise timeline of what happened, what I can verify, and what still needs confirmation. Please let me know what format or process you want me to follow."
```

## Upward Feedback

Good pattern:

```text
Do not unload the full backlog. Offer one concrete, work-focused point and test receptivity before going deeper.

You can say: "I can share one concrete thing that would help the work move faster. Would you prefer a quick example now, or a more complete version after I organize my thoughts?"
```

## Credit Misattribution

Good pattern:

```text
Restore the record calmly through contribution language, without starting with accusation.

You can say: "Just to add context for the leadership update: I drafted the analysis behind that recommendation, and [coworker] helped bring it into the final discussion. I'm happy to walk through the reasoning if useful."
```

## Decision Already Made vs. Decision Still Open

Same trigger words (manager, client, timing) can call for opposite handling,
depending on whether a decision is still open.

Prompt (decision already made — execution only):

```text
I want to give my manager a heads-up about a delay before the client asks. Can you
write me a short message?
```

Good pattern:

```text
The user has already decided to notify their manager proactively — there is no
decision left to review. Skip the procedure and provide the wording directly.

"Hi [name], wanted to give you a heads-up before the client asks: [task] will be
delayed by [time] due to [reason]. I'm working to minimize the impact and can share
an updated timeline by [date]."
```

Bad pattern:

```text
Running a full visible-issue / hidden-dynamic / public-vs-private analysis on a
request where there was never a decision to make. The trigger words are present, but
nothing about the situation is actually contested or ambiguous.
```

Prompt (decision still open):

```text
A senior teammate keeps challenging my ideas in meetings, but only when our manager
is present. I don't know whether to address it now or wait.
```

Good pattern:

```text
Here the user has not decided — the procedure applies because there is a real choice
to make (address now, wait, public vs. private). Trigger words alone did not decide
this; the open decision did.
```

## Example Quality Check

A new example should be concrete, socially complex, non-manipulative, cautious about motive, clear about timing or audience when relevant, and end with one useful next move or sentence.
